About Me

Psychology first interested me because it offered a way to ask difficult questions about why people think, feel, and behave as they do.

My research training gradually taught me another lesson: the way we measure a psychological process changes what we are able to see. Longitudinal and intensive repeated-measure approaches became especially meaningful to me because they allow us to study change rather than only differences between people.

Clinical work added another perspective. Psychological distress does not begin when an appointment starts or disappear when it ends. Much of what matters happens in ordinary life, between clinical encounters, and often when direct professional support is unavailable.

That experience increasingly motivates my interest in suicide prevention, digital mental health, and intensive longitudinal methods. I hope to study when psychological risk changes, why individuals differ in those changes, and how support can become more responsive to the moments when people need it.

How I Try to Approach Research

Curiosity

I value questions that become more interesting, rather than less interesting, the closer we look.

Methodological Care

I value methods that help distinguish compelling stories from patterns that are actually supported by evidence.

Human Meaning

Psychological data represent real experiences. I try not to lose sight of the people behind the observations.

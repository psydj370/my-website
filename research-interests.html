Research Interests

My interests center on understanding how suicide risk changes over time, how support can be individualized to the person and moment, and how digital tools can extend care when immediate human support is not available.

Research Goals

What I want my research to make possible

I want suicide prevention to move beyond identifying who is generally at risk. My goal is to help build systems that can recognize when a particular person is approaching danger, what kind of support fits that person and moment, and how care can reach them in time.

01 · Detect earlier

When does risk rise?

I want to develop idiographic, time-sensitive models using repeated assessments and digital behavioral or physiological signals to detect meaningful changes from a person's own baseline.

02 · Respond better

What support fits?

Prediction matters only if it guides an appropriate response. I want to understand which interventions work best for whom and under what conditions, including personalized coping support, safety planning, and clinician connection.

03 · Reach in time

How can care arrive when it matters?

I want to use apps and wearable technologies to extend human care into the moments between clinical contacts—providing brief support when immediate help is unavailable and connecting people to clinicians when needed.

Where My Questions Come From

One mission, built from different kinds of evidence

My interest in suicide prevention did not come from one study or one clinical encounter. It grew from living with Korea's suicide crisis, working inside an overstretched care system, studying how risk unfolds over time, and seeing how addiction can intensify vulnerability at critical moments.

My clinical work made the system-level gap concrete. Risk does not wait for an appointment, and frontline services can be overwhelmed at exactly the moments when people need them most. In 2026, Korea's Ministry of Health and Welfare reported falling response performance at the national suicide-prevention hotline, alongside counselor workload, emotional burnout, shift fatigue, and concerns about long-term retention.

My goal is not to replace human care with technology. It is to build a digital bridge before risk and human support can meet—helping people endure, stabilize, and connect to clinicians at the right moment.
03 · What my research taught me about risk

Risk is dynamic, multilevel, and unlikely to be explained by a single factor

Persongenetic & psychological vulnerability
Contextstress, relationships, environment
Timelongitudinal & within-person change
Interventionwhat works, for whom, when

Across longitudinal studies, gene–environment questions, intervention research, meta-analysis, and intensive daily data, I learned to think about psychological risk as a process rather than a label. The same vulnerability can lead to different outcomes depending on context and timing, and the same intervention may not work equally well for every person. That is why my next step is broader than prediction alone: I want to integrate person, context, time, and intervention into clinically meaningful models of suicide risk.

04 · Suicide & addiction

Addiction became my foundation because it exposed how vulnerability can become acute risk

chronic distress+ addiction→ impaired judgment / impulsivity→ more proximal suicide risk

I first approached self-destructive behavior through addiction research, and later saw the connection directly in clinical practice: suicidal crises often emerged alongside alcohol or other addictive behaviors, sometimes changing markedly once intoxication or the acute crisis had passed. The literature reinforced what I was observing. Alcohol can intensify dysphoria, cognitive dysfunction, and impulsivity, while substance misuse is consistently associated with elevated suicide mortality.

+94% Alcohol use was associated with a 94% higher risk of death by suicide in a meta-analysis of 33 longitudinal studies.
This is why I see addiction not as a separate endpoint, but as an important pathway for understanding when vulnerability may move closer to suicidal behavior.
